How an AI model is made, trained and used, with one small enough to watch. It runs in your browser and sends nothing anywhere.

Knobs

Nobody types the rules into an AI. A model is a pile of numbers, turned a hair at a time until it works. Here is one with 1,992 of them.

How do you want it explained?

The working parts are the same in all three. Only the words change.

1 Words become numbers

A model cannot read. Before anything else happens, each word you give it is swapped for a number from a fixed list. Type something about a pool and watch.

A model cannot read. Text is first cut into pieces called tokens, and each token is swapped for its number in a fixed list, the vocabulary. This model's tokens are whole words. Big models use word pieces, so a rare word becomes several tokens.

A word-level tokenizer over a 46-word vocabulary, plus a start marker and an end marker. Out-of-vocabulary words are simply unusable here. Production models use subword schemes such as byte-pair encoding, so any string can be encoded.

    2 A new model is a pile of dice rolls

    Making a model means deciding two things: how many numbers it has, and how they are wired together. Then every number is set at random. This one has 1,992. Each square below is one of them. Nothing is known yet.

    Making a model means choosing its architecture (how the numbers are wired together) and its size. The numbers are called weights, or parameters. They start out random. Everything the model will ever know ends up in them and nowhere else.

    Architecture: an 8-dimensional embedding per token; the embeddings of the two previous tokens are concatenated, passed through one tanh layer of 24 units, then a softmax over the vocabulary. 1,992 parameters, uniform random init. It is a fixed-window neural language model, the design that came before transformers.

    3 Training: guess, measure the miss, nudge

    Training is one drill, repeated. Show the model the start of a real sentence and let it guess the next word. Compare its guess with the word that was really there. Then nudge every number slightly so the right word scores a little higher next time. Nobody tells it a rule.

    Training is one drill, repeated: predict the next token, compare with the real one. The size of the miss is the loss. Working out which way each weight should move to shrink it is backpropagation, and taking that small step is gradient descent. One trip through all the examples is a pass, or epoch.

    Next-token prediction with cross-entropy loss over 434 positions from 46 sentences. Full-batch Adam, learning rate 0.02, 300 steps. The gradients are written out by hand in the script. Top-1 accuracy is capped at 298 of 434 by the entropy of the data itself.

        4 Using it: one word at a time

        A trained model writes by doing its one trick in a loop. It gives every word it knows odds of coming next, picks one, adds it, and goes again. That loop is all a chatbot is doing when the words appear one by one.

        Using a trained model is called inference. It scores every token as the next one, picks one by rolling weighted dice, appends it and repeats. The dice are why the same question can get different answers. How adventurous the roll is gets set by a dial called temperature.

        Autoregressive sampling from the softmax, with temperature. The context here is two tokens. A transformer attends over many thousands, and that is the main thing this toy lacks.

          5 Now find where it put something

          The trained model can tell you why pool water goes green. That knowledge is somewhere in these 1,992 squares, and you can see every one of them. Try to find it. Wipe a number and see what breaks.

          Every weight is in plain view, and none of them has a label. Finding what the numbers mean after training is a research field of its own, called interpretability. Wipe a weight and see what breaks.

          The representation is distributed: a fact is a direction across many weights, and each weight takes part in many facts. Ablate weights below and count how many top-1 predictions flip across the whole training set.

          No square holds one fact. Each fact is spread across many numbers, and each number does a little work for many facts. With 1,992 of them, a patient person could still untangle it. The big models have hundreds of billions.

          No weight holds one fact. In a real image model, researchers found a single unit that responds to cat faces and also to the fronts of cars. The working explanation is that a model holds more ideas than it has units, so each idea is spread across many of them. Since 2023 there have been tools that pull those ideas apart again, and they work on a small part of what a large model does.

          Single units in real models are polysemantic. The leading account is superposition: more features than dimensions, each feature a direction shared across units. Dictionary learning with sparse autoencoders recovers many interpretable features and lets you steer with them, but coverage of a large model is still partial.

          6 From this to a chatbot

          A chatbot is this same drill at a size that is hard to picture. One openly published model, Meta's Llama 3.1, has 405 billion numbers. Printed at 5,000 to a page, that is 81 million pages. It was trained on more text than a person could read in thousands of lifetimes, then trained a second time on example conversations so it answers like an assistant.

          A chatbot is the same drill at enormous scale. The first round, on a huge pile of text, is pre-training. A second round on example conversations, called fine-tuning, makes it behave like an assistant. Meta's Llama 3.1 has 405 billion weights: at 5,000 to a page, 81 million pages.

          Swap the fixed two-token window for a transformer, whose attention layers let every position read from every earlier one. Pre-train on next-token prediction over a web-scale corpus, then fine-tune on dialogue and with feedback from people. Llama 3.1 at its largest has 405 billion parameters.

          Two things people notice follow from how it is made. It can write sentences nobody has written before, because it builds them a word at a time. And it can say false things smoothly, because what was trained is what plausibly comes next. True and likely usually go together. When they do not, there is no separate step that checks.

          The people who build these can inspect every step of the arithmetic, and still cannot fully explain how it arrives at a given answer. You just met the reason at stop 5.

          What this is not

          This is a toy. It has read 46 sentences and sees only the last two words, which is why it loses its thread. Its words are whole words. It has one layer where a chatbot has dozens.

          What it shares with the big ones is the part this page is about: the numbers start random, the same drill moves them, and afterward the knowledge is in the numbers with no labels on it.

          All of it is in one script, written out by hand with no libraries. For what happens when you try to take one fact back out of a model, see Forget me.
