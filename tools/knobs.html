Knobs

Nobody typed the rules into an AI. They were turned in, a hair at a time. Here are 33 knobs to turn yourself.

Is this pool safe to swim in? Someone who knows pools can write the answer down. Most software is made this way: a person decides every step, and when the answer is wrong you can point to the line.

    4. The same trick, with words

    A chatbot has one job: given the words so far, guess the next one. Then it does that again. This guesser is as small as that job gets.

      Everything it has read

        What you just saw

        The first answer came from a rule a person wrote. You could read it, and it told you which line decided.

        The second came from 33 numbers nobody wrote. They started as dice rolls. Each nudge checked the network's answers against the 240 examples, measured how far off they were, and worked out for every knob which direction would shrink the miss. Then it turned each knob slightly that way. A few hundred rounds of that is all the training there is.

        People compare this to training a dog with treats. There is no treat here and nothing that wants one. There is a measured miss, and arithmetic that makes it smaller.

        Why the builders cannot just read it

        Every number in a model can be looked at, the way you looked at these. What is missing is a label. Move one knob on the board and several edges of the shape move at once, because the rule is held by all of them together.

        With 33 knobs, a patient person could still work it out on paper. Llama 3.1, a model Meta published in 2024, has 405 billion. Printed at 5,000 numbers to a page, that is 81 million pages.

        Researchers do open these models up. In one image model they found a single unit that fires for cat faces and also for the fronts of cars. The working explanation is that a network holds more ideas than it has units, so each idea ends up spread across many of them. Since 2023 there have been tools that pull those ideas apart again, and in 2024 one lab reported finding the pattern inside its own model that stands for the Golden Gate Bridge.

        That is real progress, and it covers a small part of what a large model does. The people who build these can inspect every step of the arithmetic and still cannot fully explain how it arrives at a given answer.

        Where those claims come from: Towards Monosemanticity (Anthropic, 2023), MIT Technology Review on mechanistic interpretability (2026), and The Neuron's summary of a 2026 research survey.

        What a chatbot adds

        A chatbot does the word guesser's job with the knob board's machinery. It reads what has been written so far, scores every word it knows as the next one, picks one, and goes again. Its knobs were trained the way these 33 were, with the next word of real text as the example to match, on far more reading than a person could do in a thousand lifetimes.

        That accounts for two things people notice. It can write sentences nobody has written before, because it builds them a word at a time. And it can say false things smoothly, because what was trained is what plausibly comes next. True and likely usually go together. When they do not, there is no separate step that checks.

        What this is not

        This is a toy. The pool rule is cut down to two readings so the picture fits on a flat page, and two readings do not make a real pool safe. The network has one layer of eight wires, and it learned a rule that was already written, which is the easy case. Networks get trained for the jobs where nobody can write the rule, like telling a cat from a car.

        The word guesser counts instead of training, and it remembers only two words back, which is why it loses its thread. A chatbot looks back over thousands of words.

        The network here ends up matching the written rule on about 98 of every 100 possible readings, not all of them. It is least reliable at the edges, where it saw the fewest examples. That part holds for the big ones too.

        All of it is in one script, written out by hand with no libraries. For what happens when you try to take something back out of the knobs, see Forget me.
